# Port proven alarms before building clever oracles

The highest-value engine HealthOS can add is the simplest one with real-world testing. It is a **two-night resting-heart-rate rule against a personal median baseline**, the Stanford NightSignal algorithm, which is public Python code you can port in an afternoon. Extend it with HRV, respiratory rate and skin temperature votes. Then do something no published system did: use the alcohol, training and travel HealthOS already logs to explain alerts away. Even in its best form this engine mostly detects *strain*, not infection. In a prospective test only **4–10% of alerts were real infections**, with a **2% per-day false-alarm rate**. So "your body looks under strain" is both the honest wording and the app's own "goes with, never diagnosis" rule. The other additions worth building soon are cheap, pure functions over data HealthOS already has. Change-point detection marks when a metric moved to a new level. A 7-day ln-RMSSD "normal band" with RHR pairing watches for training strain. Sleep regularity, sleep debt and social jetlag come from sleep times alone. The n-of-1 experiments get an upgrade from before-versus-during to randomised blocks with a "probability it helped" readout. Forecasting tomorrow's energy or mood is the most tempting addition and the least proven: published personal models beat a "personal mean" baseline for only about 8 of 11 people, no study found tests them against "same as yesterday", and the gains drift over time. Build it only behind a gate that hides any forecast that does not beat persistence on his own history. Most numbers below come from abstracts and search summaries, because nature.com, PMC, arXiv, JMIR and whoop.com were blocked during the research. The final section ranks every addition by impact against effort and names the repo or paper to start from.

## A two-night resting-HR rule is the best-tested engine, and it mostly flags strain

The illness-detection literature converges on one design. Build a robust personal baseline of overnight resting heart rate, then alert only on **sustained** deviation. Mishra et al. flagged **63% of COVID-19 cases before symptom onset** in a real-time setting with a two-tier RHR-elevation warning against a 28-day sliding baseline. The analysis was retrospective ([Nature BME](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41551-020-00640-6)). Alavi et al. then ran three real-time algorithms on 2,155 participants. NightSignal reached **80% sensitivity against 72% for CuSum and 69% for RHRAD** at a matched false-signal rate. It also fired on long flights, high altitude and other non-infectious stress ([Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-021-01593-2)).

The researchers could not read the paper's Methods. They did read the released code, and the code settles the thresholds. In **StanfordBioinformatics/wearable-infection** (Apache-2.0), overnight RHR is the average HR during zero-step minutes. Missing days are filled with the mean of their neighbours. The baseline is a running median of nightly averages. A **yellow alert needs two consecutive nights at baseline + 3 bpm, and a red alert two consecutive nights at + 4 bpm** ([repo](https://github.com/StanfordBioinformatics/wearable-infection)). The repo also ships sample Fitbit and Apple Watch CSVs, which can serve as unit-test fixtures.

The companion R code from the same lab contributes two engineering rules worth copying. **mwgrassgreen/Alarm** (MIT) restarts the baseline after a data gap of 14 days or more, builds baselines on 28-day blocks, and runs a CuSum accumulator with a p-value threshold of 0.01 ([repo](https://github.com/mwgrassgreen/Alarm)). CuSum adds up small sustained rises, so it also suits slow drifts. Duke's **CovIdentify** supplies a plain vocabulary for "how off is today": delta, normalised delta, standardised delta and z-score against baseline ([repo](https://github.com/DigitalBiomarkerDiscoveryPipeline/CovIdentify)).

Prospective use is where expectations need resetting. In the Northwell validation, 577 health-care workers enrolled and **89 never produced enough data for any prediction**. Of 512 alerts followed by a viral panel, **63 were confirmed infections**, a PPV of 4–10%. The estimated false-positive rate was 2% per day, and the authors note that alerts tracked "other physical or emotional stress events" ([JMIR Form Res 2024](https://formative.jmir.org/2024/1/e53716)).

For one man, that works out to roughly **0.6 false alerts a month**, and about one alert in 10–25 coming before a real cold. Lead time will usually be 0–3 days, shorter than the 4–7 days in retrospective press coverage ([CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/covid-symptoms-smart-watch)). The sensitivity numbers are retrospective, and no sex-specific figures were found.

The extra channels matter, and a Fitbit band has most of them. Fitbit-funded work on 2,745 PCR-confirmed cases predicted illness on a given day with **AUC 0.77** from respiratory rate up, HR up and HRV down ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/02b99d5ad127459fb9f207548d75cd8c)). Nightly respiratory rate **≥3 breaths/min above usual** appeared in 36.4% of symptomatic cases around onset ([medRxiv](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.05.15.21257200v1)).

Skin temperature was the **most important feature** in a 63,153-participant Oura fever classifier ([MDPI Sensors](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/6/1818)). But there is no validated threshold for a meaningful wearable skin-temperature deviation, and wrist skin temperature is not core temperature ([weguide.health](https://www.weguide.health/digital-biomarkers/skin-temperature)). A single warm night should therefore only ever add a vote, never trigger an alert. Combining channels also has a product precedent. Apple's Vitals notifies only when **at least two overnight metrics** are outside their typical range ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120142)). Oura's Symptom Radar grades "no / minor / major signs" and shows which inputs moved, after at least 7 nights in 14 ([Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/inside-the-ring-symptom-radar/)). Oura has published no accuracy data for it ([Tech Advisor](https://www.techadvisor.com/article/2575433/)).

HealthOS's edge over every published system is the confounder log. Alcohol is the best-quantified benign cause. In 4,098 Firstbeat users, HRV-derived overnight recovery fell by **9.3, 24.0 and 39.2 percentage points** after low (≤0.25 g/kg), moderate and high intake. The effect was dose-dependent and present within individuals even at low doses ([JMIR Mental Health](https://mental.jmir.org/2018/1/e23)). For an 80 kg man, 0.25 g/kg is about 1.5 standard drinks. Intense exercise and alcohol were named as the main sources of NightSignal false positives ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358523855_Deploying_wearable_sensors_for_pandemic_mitigation)).

An alert that says "RHR +5 bpm for two nights; you logged three drinks Friday and a leg session Saturday" is more useful than a bare warning. The gentler "goes with getting run down" line should appear only when nothing logged explains the rise. Nobody has tested whether explaining alerts away cuts false alarms, so it is the first thing to plant and check in `bench.ts`.

One more borrowed idea makes the alert measurable. **OpenHumans/quantified-flu** (MIT) shows wearable signals around a user-entered sickness date ([repo](https://github.com/OpenHumans/quantified-flu)). An "I was sick" entry in HealthOS would give Arnold his own labelled episodes. The app could then report its own hit rate and false-alarm rate on his data.

## Slow drifts need change points and HRV bands, not fitness-fatigue predictions

Illness is a sharp rise over a few nights. Overtraining, a new medication, a fitness gain or a bad month are slow level shifts that threshold rules handle badly. Two small algorithms cover these. **Bayesian online change-point detection** (BOCPD; a compact MIT implementation is [hildensia/bayesian_changepoint_detection](https://github.com/hildensia/bayesian_changepoint_detection)) can say "resting HR moved to a new level around 12 Nov" and attach a probability. **PELT** from [deepcharles/ruptures](https://github.com/deepcharles/ruptures) (BSD-2) marks regime changes on trend charts after the fact, in about 50 lines with an L2 cost. Both are method-level tools with no wellness validation. Their value is descriptive, and they need the same planted-shift tests as everything else.

For training strain, the most practical evidence supports a **7-day rolling mean of ln(RMSSD) against a personal "smallest worthwhile change" band** of ±0.5 to 1 SD ([JHSE](https://www.jhse.es/index.php/jhse/article/download/hrv-guided-training-moderate-intensity-enhances-aerobic/92/3961); [MDPI JFMK review](https://www.mdpi.com/2411-5142/6/4/102)). The case for watching it as an overtraining marker rests on very little. Plews et al. followed **two** elite triathletes. In the one who became non-functionally overreached, both the 7-day ln rMSSD and its day-to-day CV fell over the weeks before the event ([PubMed 22367011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22367011/)). Functional overreaching can also push HRV *up* through parasympathetic hyperactivity, so a rise is not automatically good ([PubMed 24136138](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24136138/)). At group level, HRV-guided training was **not significantly better** than predefined plans for VO2max or performance ([JSAMS review](https://www.jsams.org/article/S1440-2440(21)00108-0/fulltext)). No validated sensitivity or specificity exists for any overreaching rule.

The defensible feature is therefore a chart and a soft flag, not a warning. Show the 7-day mean inside or outside his band, as Garmin's HRV Status does with a roughly three-week baseline ([levlaenge](https://levlaenge.tech/en/articles/garmin-body-battery-and-hrv-status-guide/)). Raise a "strain building" note when the mean sits below the band for three or more days while training volume rises, or when RHR rises at the same time. Fitbit measures HRV during sleep rather than in the morning readings the studies used, so the direction of the logic should transfer but the thresholds might not.

Load models need the same humility. Session-RPE (a 0–10 effort rating times minutes) is supported for validity and reliability across **36 studies** ([Frontiers in Neuroscience](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2017.00612/full)). It is one tap after a workout, and it fixes the undercounting of strength sessions in HR-zone load. Apple's Training Load uses the same effort × duration scheme, with manual effort for strength ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/apde4c07a6cf)).

The acute:chronic workload ratio is contested. When the acute window sits inside the chronic one, the two are mathematically coupled, producing a spurious correlation of about 0.50 ([BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/15/921.full)). A counter-study found the coupling trivial in elite athletes ([ECU](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/7321/)). The Banister fitness-fatigue model has unstable, poorly identified individual parameters and is best used as "data-informed, not data-driven" ([arXiv 2503.14841](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14841); [Sports Med Open](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-022-00426-x)).

So the plan is: a 7-day versus uncoupled 28-day load chart with no injury claims, CTL/ATL-style curves ported from the formulas rather than from GPL [GoldenCheetah](https://github.com/GoldenCheetah/GoldenCheetah) code, and Garmin's **Training Load Focus** idea. Load Focus buckets recent load into low aerobic, high aerobic and anaerobic work and names the shortage in words ([Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/fenix66s6xpro/EN-GB/GUID-C3205D96-DAB6-4C93-A225-5B8D7B5A5621.html)). HealthOS already has HR zones, so this extends zone-2 minutes into a balance view. The target split, for example 80/15/5, is a design choice the app should state openly.

## Sleep timing is the cheapest rich signal the app is not yet mining

Sleep times alone unlock several well-defined metrics. The **Sleep Regularity Index** scores whether you are asleep or awake at the same clock minute 24 hours apart, on a scale from −100 to 100. In 61 students, DLMO in the most irregular quintile was about **2.6 hours later** than in the most regular ([PMC5468315](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5468315)). In about 61,000 UK Biobank adults, higher regularity went with **20–48% lower all-cause mortality**, and regularity predicted mortality better than duration did ([PMC10782501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10782501)).

That is population and observational evidence. For one person it supports "your sleep timing was less regular than your usual, and that goes with ..." computed on his own outcomes, never a mortality figure. It needs at least 7 days, ideally 14.

Chronotype and social jetlag come from the Munich ChronoType Questionnaire maths. The key quantity is mid-sleep on free days corrected for oversleep. [ropensci/mctq](https://github.com/ropensci/mctq) (MIT) implements it, and HealthOS can feed it tracked sleep instead of questionnaire answers. Rest-activity rhythm metrics add a "how regular is your day" companion: interdaily stability, intradaily variability, relative amplitude, and the least-active 5 hours and most-active 10 hours (L5/M10). They are a few lines each over minute steps. They come from GPL [pyActigraphy](https://github.com/ghammad/pyActigraphy), so port them from the published formulas.

Two actionable features follow. **Sleep debt**, Rise's reframing, is a 14-night rolling shortfall against a personal sleep need, and it is more actionable than a nightly score ([Rise](https://www.risescience.com/blog/best-time-to-wake-up)). Rise's own model is unpublished, so the sleep-need estimate needs a stated default, for example average sleep on alarm-free nights. A **recommended bedtime window**, as in Samsung's Bedtime Guidance ([Samsung Newsroom](https://news.samsung.com/global/new-features-on-one-ui-8-watch-help-users-build-healthier-habits)), can then ride the existing wind-down push. So can Ultrahuman's caffeine cut-off idea ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1491286709)), checked against his own caffeine-timing data with the supplement-timing engine he already has.

Estimating circadian *phase* is feasible but weakly validated. Bowman et al. fit six HR-rhythm parameters across more than 130,000 days of data and reported no DLMO accuracy figure ([PMC8462795](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8462795)). A later check found **8 of 10** lab DLMO values inside the 80% interval of the HR-estimated phase ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2021.727504/pdf)). [Arcascope/circadian](https://github.com/Arcascope/circadian) (MIT) has the light- and activity-driven models. Fitbit exposes no light data, so an activity-only version is cruder, and the claim that it uses steps as a light proxy was not re-verified in its README. Present phase only as "body clock looks earlier or later than usual".

Sleep-breathing hints are where the evidence says to tread lightest. Apple's FDA-cleared accelerometer feature has **66.3% sensitivity for AHI ≥15 and 98.5% specificity** in 1,499 subjects, and only 43.4% sensitivity for moderate apnoea ([Innolitics/FDA](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K240929); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/pulmonology/20241015/qa-advantages-pitfalls-of-sleepmonitoring-apps-trackers)). Samsung's SpO2 feature reached 82.7% sensitivity and 87.7% specificity, which missed its own prespecified criterion ([FDA DEN230041](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN230041.pdf)).

No published validation exists for HR-only screening on a Fitbit-class band. HealthOS cannot reproduce either cleared feature. The most it should say is a 30-day, specificity-first note in Apple's style. For example: frequent awakenings and nightly HR surges have been common this month, which is worth mentioning to a doctor if you also snore or feel sleepy in the day. Any wording that names apnoea edges toward a regulated claim.

## Forecasts must beat "same as yesterday" or stay hidden

The published case for personal next-day prediction is thinner than it looks. A 2026 idiographic study beat a "personal mean" baseline for daily depressive symptoms in **8 of 11 participants** with linear models. The authors warned that gains were unstable over time because the sensor–symptom link shifts ([JMIR Form Res 2026](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e99204)). An npj study reported a 65.7% error reduction against an unstated baseline ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/378586657_Personalized_mood_prediction_from_patterns_of_behavior_collected_with_smartphones/fulltext/65e08838c3b52a1170fef9fd/Personalized-mood-prediction-from-patterns-of-behavior-collected-with-smartphones.pdf)). The roughly 81% next-day mood accuracy from random forests relied on population-trained models, which a one-user app does not have ([JMIR Form Res 2023](https://formative.jmir.org/2023/1/e39425)).

Mood, energy and HRV are strongly autocorrelated. Beating the mean is easy, and beating "same as yesterday" is the real test, which none of the summarised studies reported. Google's PH-LLM work adds a sobering point: objective sleep measures predict how well people *feel* they slept only modestly ([arXiv 2406.06474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.06474)).

The right engine for one person is small and honest. Use a local-level Bayesian dynamic linear model (a Kalman filter) per target, with a weekday effect and a few lagged regressors: last night's sleep, yesterday's alcohol, training load, late caffeine. Cross-check it with a closed-form ridge regression refit weekly. This design comes from the researchers' judgement, not a benchmark. It is a few hundred lines of TypeScript. It handles missing days by skipping the update step, gives real prediction intervals, and adapts to the drift the 2026 study warns about.

Score it with walk-forward validation as skill = 1 − MAE_model / MAE_best_naive, against both persistence and an exponentially weighted personal mean. Show a forecast only while the skill is positive. How many days a personal model needs before it beats persistence is unknown, so the planted-effect bench has to answer it.

Presentation research supports showing numbers rather than adjectives. Numeric uncertainty ranges **did not reduce trust** in the number or the source, while verbal hedges did ([PNAS 2020](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1913678117); [R Soc Open Sci 2023](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/full/10.1098/rsos.230604)). "Energy tomorrow: about 3 (likely 2.5–3.5)" plus a visible track record ("within ±0.5 on 7 of the last 10 days") is the format. The evidence is general science communication, not a health-app trial.

The same lessons govern the Claude read. Google's PHIA agent writes and runs code rather than doing arithmetic in its head, and frames the task as needing tool use because numerical reasoning trips up standard LLMs ([arXiv 2406.06464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06464v3)). The later Personal Health Agent splits work into data-science, domain-expert and coach roles ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/the-anatomy-of-a-personal-health-agent/)). Its code is Apache-2.0 and includes an Anthropic backend and prompt files.

The most directly reusable asset is PH-LLM's released sleep and fitness **case studies**. Each pairs sensor input with expert etiology, insight and recommendation answers and a rating rubric ([google-health/consumer-health-research](https://github.com/google-health/consumer-health-research); [Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03888-0)). They make an offline regression test for morning-read prompt changes. The dataset licence was not checked.

Commercial coaches show what to avoid. Whoop users complain of forgetting and invented data ([Whoop community](https://www.community.whoop.com/t/whoop-coach-ai-is-getting-worse/16360)). Fitbit's Gemini coach "latches on to any context you give it" and pushes relentless improvement ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/ai-platforms-assistants/fitbits-gemini-ai-coach-is-giving-users-unhinged-fitness-advice-heres-why-users-are-saying-they-cannot-wait-for-my-trial-to-end)). Whoop's answer worth copying is a memory the user can view, edit and delete ([Whoop](https://www.whoop.com/us/en/thelocker/my-memory-whoop/)).

## Randomisation, not cleverer statistics, is the causal upgrade

HealthOS already has the hard part: experiments that end themselves. The biggest gain is in design, not in the estimator. A before-versus-during comparison mixes the treatment up with drift, season and life events. Randomly ordered A/B blocks remove that. The classic ABAB alternation and its power under autocorrelation and carryover are well studied ([PMC6787650](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6787650)).

"Analytic washout", dropping the first days of each block rather than pausing treatment, keeps trials short ([arXiv 2412.15076](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15076)). A Bayesian analysis then gives the direct readout people understand, such as "82% chance it helps". It can model trend, carryover and autocorrelation ([PMC10817775](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10817775)). [StudyU](https://github.com/hpi-studyu/studyu) (MIT, active) is a good model for phase lengths, washout and randomised block order. The stale Bayesian n-of-1 R packages such as [cinof1](https://github.com/HIAlab/cinof1) show the model: a normal outcome with a treatment effect and AR(1) errors under weak priors. NAP (non-overlap of all pairs, "on 78% of day-pairs B beat A") from [SingleCaseES](https://github.com/jepusto/SingleCaseES) or [scan](https://github.com/jazznbass/scan) makes a robust, explainable second readout. Port these from formulas: scan is GPL and SingleCaseES has no root licence file.

For changes that were not randomised, such as starting creatine or a new job, segmented regression (interrupted time series) with a level change, a slope change and AR(1) errors is cheap. [CausalPy](https://github.com/pymc-labs/CausalPy) (Apache-2.0) is the reference. Aim for 3–4 weeks of daily data on each side; that is a rule of thumb the researchers could not source. CausalImpact-style structural time series **assume control series the change did not affect** ([Brodersen et al.](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/41854.pdf)). One person rarely has them: weather qualifies, steps or sleep do not. That makes it easy to misuse.

Daza's counterfactual framing of self-tracked series is best treated as a hypothesis generator ([PMC6087468](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6087468)). So is MoTR's "model twin" ([arXiv 2208.00739](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.00739v4)). Their output should be "this goes with better sleep; want to test it?", which feeds the experiment engine. Granger causality, transfer entropy and causal-discovery tools such as [causal-learn](https://github.com/py-why/causal-learn) should not ship. With a few dozen effective observations they will mostly find noise and weekends. The existing lagged correlations with FDR already cover that ground with honest error control.

Two cheap trust upgrades apply to every correlation. Whoop's Journal unlocks a behaviour only after **5 "yes" and 5 "no" days within 90**, and warns that other behaviours may confound the estimate ([Whoop](https://www.whoop.com/ca/en/thelocker/a-new-way-to-see-insights-on-which-behaviors-affect-your-recovery/)). HealthOS should show the same gate in words ("needs 3 more days without alcohol") and carry the same confounding caveat. Exist correlates the *absence* of tags as well as their presence ([Exist](https://exist.io/about/custom-tags/)), which is worth checking in HealthOS's engine. Google's synthetic wearable log generator with planted cause-effect links ([repo](https://github.com/google-health/consumer-health-research)) is a ready source of extra planted and null cases for `bench.ts`.

## Notes become symptom tags only with a quote and a confirm tap

LLM symptom extraction is accurate on clean, explicit text. It reached **F1 0.968–0.983** on 700 standardised exam notes ([JMIR Med Inform 2025](https://medinform.jmir.org/2025/1/e78432)). It degrades sharply on subtle symptoms: no model passed macro-F1 0.6 on psychotic-disorder symptoms, and **misses outnumbered inventions** ([medRxiv 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.16.26363090v1.full)). Casual diary notes were not studied and are likely harder still.

That error profile dictates the pipeline. Map notes onto a fixed, user-editable vocabulary of plain feeling words ("headache", "bloated", "low back sore"). Require each extracted item to carry a quote, and **reject any item whose quote is not literally in the note**, a deterministic guard against intrinsic hallucination ([arXiv 2410.18460](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.18460)). Show the results as suggestions to confirm, and let him add tags by hand, since misses are the main failure. Filter disease names in code. Nomie's inline syntax (`#coffee(2) +work`) from the MIT-licensed [nomie6-oss](https://github.com/open-nomie/nomie6-oss) is a proven way to let him tag deliberately, as well.

Analysis then reuses existing machinery. Merge tagged days within a 1–2 day gap into episodes. Compare the 1–3 days before each onset with matched non-onset days, using the same FDR and "goes with" wording, and say "not enough headaches yet" when events are rare. The regulatory line is about intended purpose and claims, not mechanism. That holds under the FDA's revised General Wellness guidance of January 2026 ([Covington](https://cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)) and under EU MDCG 2019-11 Rev. 1 ([EC](https://ec.europa.eu/docsroom/documents/37581/attachments/1/translations/en/renditions/native)).

"Headache days went with nights under 6 h (8 of 11)" stays wellness. "You may have migraine" does not. A neutral "if this is new or severe, check with a doctor" for a short red-flag list, such as chest pain or fainting, is reasonable. No source explicitly blesses it, though, and the FDA text itself was read only through law-firm summaries.

## Track a few passive or time-boxed things, not more daily sliders

The added inputs with the best payoff explain sleep, because sleep is measured nightly and gives the within-person models their power. Daylight has the strongest observational evidence. In 86,772 UK Biobank adults with wrist light sensors, more daytime light and less night light went with lower odds of depression and several other disorders ([Nature Mental Health](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44220-023-00135-8.pdf)). Ordinary evening light around **25 lux** half-suppressed melatonin on average, with individual sensitivity spanning **6 to 350 lux** ([PMC6575863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6575863)). That fifty-fold spread is exactly the kind of individual effect a personal experiment can find.

The catch is capture. A Fitbit band exposes no light stream, and a PWA cannot read the phone's light sensor. Apple's automatic daylight minutes ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/apd3ab22534c/watchos)) do not apply here. The practical versions are a one-tap "outside this morning" log and a two-week "dim lights after 21:00" experiment.

Bedroom air and heat are passive once a sensor is bought, though the evidence comes from small studies. Lower CO2 (~835 vs ~2,395 ppm) improved next-day sleepiness and test performance in a DTU dormitory study ([DTU Orbit](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/bedroom-ventilation-and-sleep-quality-2/)). A 23-bedroom intervention found shorter deep sleep under low ventilation ([PubMed 37142023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37142023/)). In older adults, sleep efficiency fell a clinically relevant **5–10% from 25 to 30 °C** ([PubMed 37474050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37474050/)). Which consumer monitors expose an API was not researched.

Session-RPE and a morning soreness slider cost one tap each and feed the load engine above. Slow breathing near six breaths a minute is one of the few interventions with meta-analytic support for **large reductions in self-reported stress (g = 0.83)** ([Psychological Medicine](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/effect-of-heart-rate-variability-biofeedback-training-on-stress-and-anxiety-a-metaanalysis/A839E9C968E54774DF5C8FB186764EF0)). It suits a logged practice or an in-app pacer tested through the experiment engine.

Two measurements are best done in periodic blocks rather than daily. **Home blood pressure** is preferred over office readings in the 2024 ESC guideline, which also added an "elevated" band from 120/70 ([ESC](https://www.escardio.org/Guidelines/Clinical-Practice-Guidelines/All-ESC-Practice-Guidelines/Elevated-Blood-Pressure-and-Hypertension)). But self-monitoring *alone* moved systolic BP by only −1.0 mmHg (not significant); the benefit came from acting on the readings ([PubMed 28926573](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28926573/)). A quarterly BP week, analysed against his logged alcohol, sodium and training, is the right shape for it.

**Labs** belong in a results table with units and trends. The one verified recommendation is to measure Lp(a) once in adult life ([ESC/EAS 2025](https://www.escardio.org/Guidelines/Clinical-Practice-Guidelines/All-ESC-Practice-Guidelines/Dyslipidaemias)). Guidance on ApoB, HbA1c, ferritin, vitamin D, testosterone and the rest was not researched and needs its own pass. Unit conversion is the real correctness risk in that table.

A 3-minute phone PVT is valid for detecting sleep-loss impairment ([Behav Res Methods](https://dx.doi.org/10.3758/s13428-016-0763-8)). Use it only during experiments, because browser touch latency varies by device.

A CGM is the clearest skip. Reviews find no evidence it improves health in people without diabetes ([Gothenburg](https://www.gu.se/en/news/no-evidence-that-continuous-glucose-monitors-improve-health-in-people-without-diabetes)). A 2026 meta-analysis found no glycaemic benefit in normoglycaemic people ([PubMed 41588451](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41588451/)). A ~20% device error can turn normal glucose into apparent lows and spikes ([UCL](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10195783/1/Diabetic%20Medicine%20-%202024%20-%20Oganesova%20-%20Innovative%20solution%20or%20cause%20for%20concern%20%20The%20use%20of%20continuous%20glucose%20monitors%20in.pdf)). One 14-day sensor to rank his own meals is the most it justifies.

Every new daily metric also feeds tracking anxiety. "Orthosomnia" describes patients whose sleep worsened while chasing tracker numbers, though the evidence is case reports ([PubMed 27855740](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27855740/)). Passive capture and time-boxed blocks are the defaults.

## Leading apps are worth copying for their gates and words, not their scores

Independent validation exists for raw overnight signals, not for composite scores. In 536 nights against ECG, Oura Gen 4 agreed best on overnight HRV (CCC 0.99), with Whoop at 0.94 and Garmin at 0.87 ([Physiological Reports](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.14814/phy2.70527)). No readiness or recovery score was validated. Apple's new Readiness had no white paper as of October 2026 ([Kygo](https://www.kygo.app/post/apple-watch-readiness-score)). Whoop Age's only validation is Whoop's own white paper ([Whoop](https://assets.ctfassets.net/rbzqg6pelgqa/3ONehqJslbqxI7CQlwGjfT/36429d6f66940e1fd866a772ed5bfc93/WHOOP_2025_White_Paper_Healthspan__6_.pdf)).

So what transfers is design: when to stay silent, how to name contributors, and which words to use.

## Where the numbers came from, and which to recheck

The research sessions could not fetch full text from **nature.com, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, arxiv.org, formative.jmir.org, whoop.com or dcrainmaker.com**, and the GitHub REST API was blocked. As a result, nearly every study figure here comes from abstracts, publisher snippets, FDA database extracts or search summaries. That covers Alavi's 80/72/69%, Mishra's 63%, the Northwell PPV and false-positive rate, Natarajan's AUC, Pietilä's alcohol gradient, the SRI mortality ranges, the forecasting studies and the Whoop/Oura details.

Several figures came from secondary sources only and should be checked before any user-facing use. These are Mishra's 4–7 day lead time (press coverage), NightSignal's ~0.08 false positives per healthy user (ResearchGate), the HRV-guided effect sizes, Garmin readiness weights, Oura's "+0.2 °C" temperature claim, and the year of the 30.9% orthosomnia abstract. PHIA's and Health-LLM's accuracy tables were never retrieved, and Whoop Age's coefficients and Symptom Radar's thresholds are unpublished.

The **verified** items are the repositories. Each was shallow-cloned, and its last-commit date, licence and, where noted, code were read directly. That is how NightSignal's 3/4 bpm, two-night thresholds and Alarm's 14-day gap rule were confirmed. Star counts were verified only from a GitHub topic page, and several "HealthOS take" notes on repos (Arcascope's models, open-wearables' provider list) were written from memory rather than re-read.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the question from "which smarter model?" to "which honest gate?". Every engine with real validation behind it is a simple personal-baseline rule. Every sophisticated one, whether forecasts, causal discovery, Banister prediction, composite ages or AI coaches, either lacks single-person validation or has documented failure modes. HealthOS's distinctive asset is not a better algorithm. It is the confounder log and the planted-effect bench, which together let it do what no published system did: explain its own alerts, and measure its own false-alarm rate and forecast skill on one person's history before showing anything.

That suggests a single architectural rule for everything that follows. Each new engine ships with a replay over his past data and a stated hit rate, false-alarm rate or skill score. It stays silent until that number clears a threshold. Its output is phrased as "goes with", plus whatever logged events explain it. Built this way, even weak-evidence features such as circadian phase or breathing hints become safe to try, because the app reports how well they work for him rather than borrowing a population's claim.
